Chemin du Fleuve earns its name: opened as early as 1738, it runs along a watercourse whose
daily rhythm, the tide, explains much of what sets Saint-Romuald apart from sectors bordering
only an inland river. Few sectors in the Communauté métropolitaine de Québec can trace their
oldest building stock along a route nearly as old as the colony itself.

## A road older than the country

The village core of Saint-Romuald formed along chemin du Fleuve, opened in 1738 and extended
west in 1798, past côte Rouge, to reach what would become secteur New-Liverpool. The earlier
stretch, further east, forms secteur Etchemin. The fieldstone houses of both stretches, built
around the église de Saint-Romuald and its presbytère raised in 1855-1856 to the plans of
Charles Baillairgé, never received a perimeter drain: the technique simply did not exist yet
when they were built.

## What the river's tide keeps up

Unlike a sector bordering an inland river with a relatively stable flow, the river in front of
Saint-Romuald rises and falls twice a day. That tidal swing keeps the base of the stone
foundations continuously wet, speeding up the breakdown of the lime mortar between fieldstones
rather than failing a pipe — since there was never a pipe to fail in this old core.

## A protected sector, not a frozen one

The PPU Vieux-Saint-Romuald governs the appearance of work on buildings within the heritage
core, but it does not exempt anyone from a technical check. Before authorising work near chemin
du Fleuve or Pointe Benson, note the date and exact location of an infiltration, photograph it
with a ruler for scale, and have an inspection confirm whether the origin is the mortar of an
old foundation or a more recent issue specific to the sector's rare younger construction. The
inspection report remains the document that matters, regardless of what the zoning plan
protects on the surface.
