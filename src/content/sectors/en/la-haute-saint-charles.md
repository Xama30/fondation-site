Four distinct boroughs merged into today's La Haute-Saint-Charles over successive municipal
amalgamations. Loretteville, Val-Bélair, Saint-Émile and the Lac-Saint-Charles area each kept
the construction pace inherited from their own history, and that separate municipal past is
what explains why the housing stock is not uniform from one end of the territory to the other.

## Four boroughs folded into one

Loretteville keeps an older core near its historic centre, while Val-Bélair and the edges of
Lac-Saint-Charles were mostly built after 1970, on large wooded lots carved out along the edge
of forested land. Saint-Émile sits somewhere in between the two. That diversity rules out
treating the borough as a single dated construction wave, unlike an area built all at once in
the 1960s.

## Crawl spaces, PVC drains and woodland moisture

Most homes built after 1970 sit on a crawl space rather than a full basement, a difference that
changes both access and inspection: you rarely get to stand upright down there. The perimeter
drain from that generation is corrugated PVC, more flexible than the clay tile laid elsewhere in
the region, but just as vulnerable to roots from the large trees bordering these wooded lots. A
stump left in place after a cut keeps rotting against the footing for years, and the moisture it
holds, combined with what the tree cover retains around Marais du Nord, keeps soil moisture
higher for longer than on open ground.

## Before calling a contractor

The useful reflex stays documentary: pinpoint the exact spot of a leak or a damp smell, note
whether the episode follows a firewood cut or heavy rain, and insist on a camera inspection
before authorising any excavation. With a crawl space, where access is already tighter, this
step matters twice as much — it keeps a footing from being opened up on a guess alone.
