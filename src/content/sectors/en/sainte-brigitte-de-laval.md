Sainte-Brigitte-de-Laval owes its "little Switzerland" nickname to a mountainous relief that
shapes foundation pressure here directly, though not in the way you might expect from a
municipality built largely after the year 2000.

## A recent boom, in the wrong spot

Between 2009 and 2013, the municipality's population doubled, adding a substantial stock of
poured-concrete foundations and PVC drains built to the BNQ 3661-500 standard along avenue
Sainte-Brigitte and rue des Monardes. Construction that recent usually lowers pressure
elsewhere on the north shore. Here, it sits right at the bottom of the Montmorency valley,
near rue du Ruisseau — the spot where the water table runs highest, regardless of the drain's
compliance.

## What the relief changes

The valley that gives the municipality its nickname acts like a funnel: runoff from the
slopes converges toward the hollow, which is exactly where the newer housing stock
concentrates. A new, well-laid drain absorbs that load without incident in most cases. But the
margin for error is thinner than on flat ground, and a drain that starts to weaken shows it
faster than it would elsewhere.

## The upper valley, a different story

The upper valley, more forested and reached by secondary roads rather than avenue
Sainte-Brigitte, largely escaped the 2009-2013 wave. Its housing stock is more scattered and
often older, raising different questions than the valley floor — less a matter of a
widespread water table than of individual foundations to be assessed one by one.

## Before authorising any work

A newer, compliant drain does not remove the need for an inspection in this sector. Document
the date of each infiltration, its relation to rain or snowmelt, and the exact spot in the
house. A written inspection report remains the only way to know whether the cause is the
valley position or an isolated drain defect.
