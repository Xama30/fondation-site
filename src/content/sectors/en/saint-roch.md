Saint-Roch does not resemble any of its immediate neighbours. The district started out
industrial, with warehouses and factories built in the late nineteenth century along rue
Saint-Joseph and boulevard Charest, before becoming, since the 1990s, one of the most densely
converted areas in the borough.

## A factory's foundation, not a house's

A converted industrial warehouse sits on a stone foundation built to carry solid walls and
heavy floor loads, not to receive the many openings a residential or office building
requires. That foundation never had a drain, as elsewhere in the borough, but the problem
layered on top of it is specific to Saint-Roch.

## What the conversion into lofts changed

Turning a factory into lofts and offices required cutting new shared service shafts through a
foundation that never needed them, to run plumbing and wiring for each unit. Each shaft
breaks the wall's continuity at that exact point, opening a path for water the original
industrial building, with its solid walls, never had. This has nothing to do with a clogged
drain on rue Dorchester or elsewhere: it is a deliberate opening cut into solid stone, not an
underground pipe reaching the end of its life after decades buried in the ground.

## Living above a shop

Ground-floor commercial space now dominates rue Saint-Joseph, with restaurants, cafés and
studios occupying former workshops, all the way to Marché du Vieux-Port. For anyone living in
a loft upstairs, a basement leak often affects the whole building before it affects one unit
in particular, which changes who needs to be consulted before an inspection is authorised —
the condo board rather than a single occupant, steps from the site of the ancien Mail
Saint-Roch. A crack noticed near a shared shaft is worth photographing with a ruler for scale
and dating precisely, since that record helps an inspector trace whether the water follows
the shaft or a separate path through the stone.
