Saint-Émile is an almost entirely residential suburb, single-family homes nearly to the last
one, on land recorded as the concession de Robert Giffard (1647) and left wooded well after the
first houses appeared.

## A suburb ringed by forest

The couvert forestier around Saint-Émile has never been pushed back beyond the rear yards, a
rarer situation than it sounds for a suburb this size. The paroisse de Saint-Émile (1925) and
the bureau de poste Saint-Émile-de-Québec (1904) are reminders that the area existed well
before the single-family housing boom that followed the Second World War.

## Roots and clay tile, a precise mechanical problem

The bungalows built between 1945 and 1975 along avenue Lapierre and rue de la Faune nearly all
received the same clay tile drain, laid in short sections. With forest right at the property
lines, tree roots seek out the moisture that tile drain releases at its joints, work their way
in, and eventually block the flow. That is a different mechanism from a high water table: the
cause comes from above, not below, which is why a camera inspection here looks for root
intrusion first rather than for a sagging or crushed section.

## What the golf course marks, and what it doesn't

To the south, the terrain de golf and the zone tampon boisée beside it, under a ligne
électrique, separate the residential stock from land that will stay unbuilt. That boundary
shapes the neighbourhood, but it changes nothing for drainage: the factor that matters is the
forest bordering yards directly, not the golf course itself.

## What to document before any work begins

The only useful step is to observe and document: the date of the water intrusion, its exact
location, a photo with a ruler for scale. A camera inspection then confirms whether a root has
actually broken through the drain, before a contractor proposes anything.
