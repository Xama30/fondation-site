Loretteville has no geometric centre, unlike some other village cores in the region. It grew
along a single commercial street, rue Racine, inherited from the leather trade that sustained
the former town before it merged into Québec in 2002.

## A tannery street that became a village street

Rue Racine once ran past tanneries, and the houses that settled along it reflect that
commercial past: mansard-roofed houses, industrial vernacular houses and Boomtown-style houses,
built before 1945 to hold a business on the ground floor rather than a single family. The name
Jeune-Lorette, older still, is a reminder that this territory had its own identity long before
becoming a Québec neighbourhood.

## Foundations built for a cellar, not a basement

That commercial past shapes the foundation itself. The houses on rue Racine often sit on
rubble-stone footings dug shallow, enough for a cool cellar but not for a livable basement by
today's standard. It is the lime mortar between the stones, not a reinforced concrete slab,
that reacts to freeze-thaw cycles.

## What sets the core apart from the rest of Loretteville

Around boulevard Bastien, boulevard de l'Ormière and rue Saint-Romain, the building stock
shifts in age and technique. Those roads, and the ones leading toward avenue du
Lac-Saint-Charles or avenue de la Montagne, serve newer development on concrete rather than
stone: the old core along rue Racine stays a concentrated exception, not the sector's norm. A
house a few streets over from Racine is more likely to share a foundation type with the rest of
the region than with its own historic centre.

## What a stone foundation requires before any work

The useful step stays documentary: photograph the affected mortar with a ruler for scale, note
the date and conditions, and insist on a written diagnosis before authorising any work on a
stone foundation, which is more delicate to handle than a concrete one.
