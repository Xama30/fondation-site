borough. That past still sets the neighbourhood apart from the rest of the borough, with its
own street layout and housing stock, built around boulevard des Chutes and boulevard

## A former town, now a neighbourhood

From 1913 to 1976, Giffard existed as a separate municipality, before merging into what would
become the Beauport borough. The name outlived the merger and now describes this specific
neighbourhood, still recognisable by its street layout and quarter-acre lots, distinct from the
rest of the borough.

## Three generations of footings on a handful of streets

The first housing stock, quarter-acre bungalows raised in the 1960s, still runs its original
clay tile drain in long sections stretching to the berge de la rivière Beauport, along the Parc
linéaire de la Rivière Beauport. The longer the run, the more joints it carries, and it is
there, near the river, that they fail first. The 1970s split-level homes filling in these
streets add a half-level footing: a structural joint more exposed to differential movement
than the ordinary vertical shrinkage crack of a neighbouring bungalow. The 1980s row housing,
built later still, closes out the sequence with party walls that limit access around the
footing.

## Before authorising an excavation

Chemin Royal, boulevard des Chutes, rue Saint-Samuel: these streets tell a story of gradual
infill, not one dated construction wave as in some other neighbourhoods of the borough. The
same symptom — a basement crack, a damp smell — does not call for the same diagnosis
depending on whether you live in a 1960s bungalow, a split-level or a row house a few streets
over. The owner's role stays documentary in all three cases: pinpoint where the water enters,
note the date and whether the episode follows heavy rain or snowmelt, photograph it with a
ruler, and insist on a camera inspection before authorising an excavation. On a half-level
footing as much as on a long run of clay tile, the inspection report is what separates a
cleaning from a full rebuild.
