Pintendre doesn't read like a neighbourhood built all at once. It's a former rural municipality,
now part of the city of Lévis, whose stock stretches along a single road rather than clustering
by construction era.

## A road longer than the sector itself

Avenue des Ruisseaux, the longest street in the city of Lévis, runs the length of Pintendre's
farmland north to south and crosses the rivière à la Scie at several points. Two neighbouring
properties on that same avenue can therefore sit at very different distances from a watercourse,
depending on exactly where they were built — a geographic fact, not an indication of soil type.

## An old farm concession around chemin Pintendre

Near chemin Pintendre and the église Saint-Louis-de-Pintendre, the stock is still often that of an
old farm concession: stone footings predating 1945, never connected to a perimeter drain because
the technique did not exist when they were built. The sector also carries the memory of a
moulin seigneurial (1706), founded by seigneur Georges Regnard Duplessis, a historical marker of
how old the settlement is that says nothing about the condition of any one footing.

## Toward the Appalachians, newer construction

Climbing toward the Appalachian foothills, away from the former white pine stand that once
covered the territory, the stock changes character: newer houses, poured in concrete after 2005,
with a PVC drain rather than clay tile or undrained stone. That mix of two very different building
stocks across one spread-out territory is why no level here can go past moderate: the real
pressure depends on the exact spot, not on an average for the whole sector.

Before authorising an excavation, the starting point stays the same everywhere across the
Desjardins borough: date a sign, photograph it with a ruler, and demand a written inspection
report rather than assume a cause.
