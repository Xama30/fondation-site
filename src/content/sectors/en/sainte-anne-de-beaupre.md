Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré grew in two stages around the same anchor point: the basilique
Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré, a major pilgrimage site for generations, near where rivière
Sainte-Anne-du-Nord meets fleuve Saint-Laurent.

## Two waves of construction, one place

The oldest residential core — stone houses built before 1945 around the basilica — predates the
rise of religious tourism that followed. Later, inns, motels and guesthouses were added along
avenue Royale to house pilgrims and visitors, generally on younger foundations. Two waves, two
footing ages, and two upkeep needs that have nothing in common.

## What the river adds to the old core

The mouth of rivière Sainte-Anne-du-Nord keeps the water table near the surface around the village
core for much of the year. On a lime-mortar stone footing with no original drain, water sitting
against the wall wears the joints faster than it would on drier ground. The presbytery and the
homes closest to the basilica face this combination most directly.

## Telling an old wall apart from a recent commercial building

The useful step differs by building age. On an old stone house, it means watching for a joint
that hollows out rather than a vertical crack. On a newer motel or inn, the question is more
about the condition of an existing drain than its absence. Either way, noting the date and
location of each damp episode, and insisting on an inspection before authorising work, remains
the common starting point.

An inn operator has an extra reason to document the building's condition compared with the owner
of a private home: a basement that keeps taking on water can affect rented rooms, not just a
storage space, and a guest's complaint travels faster than a private owner's patience with a
recurring problem. The inspection report then becomes a concrete point of comparison between
quotes, rather than a formality squeezed between two waves of pilgrims passing through town.
