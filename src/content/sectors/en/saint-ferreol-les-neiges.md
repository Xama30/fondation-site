Saint-Ferréol-les-Neiges is not one uniform block: this rural municipality groups two hamlets,
Saint-Léon and Saint-Nicolas, tucked at the foot of mont Sainte-Anne and mont Ferréol, near Canyon
Sainte-Anne and Sept-Chutes.

## An artificial lake that changes the pressure on the drain

Lac des Trois Castors, in Saint-Nicolas, is not a natural body of water: it was created in 1954,
and its maintained level keeps the water table near the surface for the chalets around it. Those
chalets, built on concrete-block footings tied to a clay-tile drain typical of that cohort, now
face a drain nearing the end of its service life — a pressure the lake itself sustains, unlike
ground that simply drains downhill.

## From seasonal chalet to year-round home

Many properties around lac des Trois Castors moved from weekend use to permanent occupancy
without the footing catching up to that shift. A building designed for a few months a year was
not always waterproofed to the standard of a home occupied continuously, and constant heating
reveals weaknesses that stayed hidden through decades of seasonal life.

## Terrain that sets the hamlets apart

Between Canyon Sainte-Anne, Sept-Chutes and the slopes of mont Ferréol, the municipality's rugged
terrain makes natural drainage vary sharply from one wooded lot to the next. A chalet set back,
with no body of water nearby, does not face the same constant pressure as a property on lac des
Trois Castors. Documenting the date and conditions of each damp episode, before insisting on an
inspection, remains the starting point regardless of the hamlet.

Comparing a quote written for a Saint-Nicolas property against one for a Saint-Léon chalet makes
little sense without accounting for this difference in pressure on the water table: two
similar-looking chalets built the same decade can call for very different work depending on
whether an artificial lake keeps water close to their footing or not.
