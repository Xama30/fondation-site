Wendake sits on a narrow territory along rivière Saint-Charles, near chute Kabir Kouba, entirely
enclosed within Québec's urban fabric. Two building stocks coexist there, born centuries apart,
and a wet basement does not call for the same explanation depending on where you stand.

## A housing program that produced an entire cohort at once

North of Vieux-Wendake, most homes stem from a housing program launched by the Conseil de la
Nation huronne-wendat in 1970, which built a ring of bungalows on poured-concrete foundations
with corrugated PVC drains. That cohort, built within a few years, ages at roughly the same pace
across the sector. The proximity of rivière Saint-Charles and chute Kabir Kouba keeps the water
table high for much of the year: as long as the drain works, the house shows nothing; the moment
a section sags or clogs, water stays in contact with the foundation longer than it would
elsewhere.

## Vieux-Wendake, a building stock that never had a drain

Along rue du Chef-Thomas-Martin and rue Chef Max-Gros-Louis, in Vieux-Wendake, recognized as a
historic district, the buildings are older and rest on stone foundations. Foundation drains did
not exist as a technique when they were built, and what degrades in those walls is the mortar
between the fieldstones, exposed to water passing through rather than a pipe giving way.
Properties near the riverbank, toward Onhoüa Chetek8e or Hôtel-Musée des Premières Nations, are
no exception: proximity to water remains a geographic marker, not a diagnosis, and each
foundation needs to be assessed on its own terms.

The right response does not change across the territory: date every episode of dampness,
photograph it with a ruler for scale, and insist on an inspection report that tells a drain
problem apart from a mortar problem. On Wendake territory, permit questions fall to the Conseil
de la Nation huronne-wendat, not a neighbouring Québec borough.
