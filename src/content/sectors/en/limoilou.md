Limoilou changes face faster than its footing does. The working-class triplexes clustered
around 3e Avenue and 1re Avenue, built in the early twentieth century on a stone footing with
no drain, have drawn young families and young professionals for several years now — buyers
who renovate, but not always who verify.

## A renovation that stops at the footing

The most common renovation here touches the inside: a redone kitchen, sanded floors, walls
opened up. The exterior staircase, the brick façade and, underneath, the original footing
stay untouched from one generation of owners to the next. A footing never reopened is also a
footing never reassessed: nobody knows precisely what condition the stone is in beneath an
otherwise complete renovation.

## Rivière Saint-Charles, right next door

Rivière Saint-Charles runs along the neighbourhood and, despite renaturalised banks that have
improved how surface water is managed along the trails, keeps a water table high against
unmodernised foundations from one end of Limoilou to the other. It is the combination of the
two factors — a footing never reopened and a water table that stays high — that sets
Limoilou apart from areas further south in the borough, where the footing is similar but the
water table sits lower.

## 3e Avenue and 1re Avenue, a road that became a neighbourhood

3e Avenue and 1re Avenue follow the line of the ancien chemin de Charlesbourg, and it is
along this axis that the density of working-class triplexes runs highest, out toward 5e Rue.
Near église Saint-Charles-de-Limoilou, a ruelle verte now runs through some blocks, and
stretches of the street turn into a rue piétonne l'été — a summer pedestrian street — none of
which says anything about the footing hidden beneath the exterior staircases that give the
whole neighbourhood so much of its character today.
