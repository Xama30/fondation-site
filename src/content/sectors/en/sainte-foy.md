Sainte-Foy is a different case from the older stone sectors nearby: what dominates here isn't the
age of the housing stock but the fact that most of it was rebuilt very recently, around an
institutional and commercial hub rather than a village core.

## A dominant stock born after 2005

Boulevard Laurier, Université Laval and the Laurier Québec shopping centre have drawn, since the
early 2000s, a wave of high-rise construction: condo towers, office towers, student housing. These
buildings sit on poured-concrete foundations meeting the BNQ 3661-500 standard and shared utility
shafts serving several units, an engineering reality that has nothing to do with a single-family
house's footing. It's this recent stock that dominates the sector's data and explains a lower
pressure level than in the stone neighbourhoods nearby.

## What that picture doesn't show

Behind the aire de restauration and the towers lining boulevard Laurier, quieter residential
streets remain set back toward the river, around route de l'Église. These are postwar houses,
built between 1945 and 1975, with their own clay-tile drain installed at construction. That drain
has nothing to do with the recent standard of the nearby towers: it is now approaching the end of
its useful life, a fact that concerns this specific pocket, not the sector as a whole.

## Two building stocks, two timelines

A single sector can therefore hold two opposite realities: a recent institutional and commercial
stock under low pressure, and an older residential pocket whose drain is nearing its limit.
Mixing the two up leads either to underestimating a real problem near route de l'Église, or to
worrying needlessly about a tower built to today's standards.

## Before authorising any work

The starting point stays the same regardless of the stock's age: date any sign observed,
photograph it with a ruler, and demand a written inspection report before authorising work,
whether the property sits near the CHUL or on an older street of Sainte-Foy.
