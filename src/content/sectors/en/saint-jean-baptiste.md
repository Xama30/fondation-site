Saint-Jean-Baptiste does not share the low-rise stock of its working-class neighbours. Along
rue Saint-Jean and avenue Honoré-Mercier, rental buildings climb three, four, sometimes five
storeys, built between 1880 and 1920 on rubble-stone footings with no perimeter drain. That
height, more than the age of the stock, is what sets the neighbourhood apart from its
immediate neighbours.

## A vertical density the rest of the borough does not have

A footing here carries more weight than elsewhere in the borough: each added storey of a
rental building loads the lime mortar between the fieldstones with weight it was never
formulated to absorb over a hundred and forty years. It is not the drain that fails first,
since there was never one to begin with — it is the mortar joint that eventually gives way
under a combination of weight and moisture the low houses of Saint-Sauveur or Limoilou do not
face in the same proportions.

## A slope that decides who takes the water

The neighbourhood is not flat. From côte Sainte-Geneviève, the ground drops toward boulevard
René-Lévesque, and that grade sends runoff toward buildings at the low end of the slope
rather than spreading it evenly. A footing on avenue De Salaberry or near rue de
Claire-Fontaine, further down, absorbs a volume of water the same mortar, higher up the hill,
never sees.

## One footing, one shared diagnosis

On rue Jacques-Parizeau as on rue Saint-Jean, most of these buildings house several
households under one roof and one footing. A basement leak rarely affects a single tenant: it
is the condo board or the building's owner who has to commission a report on the footing
before authorising work, one that documents the full perimeter rather than a single wall. Rue
Saint-Jean, a rue piétonne l'été each summer, stays a busy commercial strip where occupied
storefronts complicate access to certain façades during the warm months.
