Beauport is not a uniform area, and that is the first thing to understand before reading a
quote. The borough spans three centuries of construction within a few kilometres, from the old
core backing onto the river to the subdivisions that reach autoroute Félix-Leclerc.

## Two building stocks, two different problems

Along avenue Royale and through Vieux-Bourg, the houses are stone and predate 1945. They have no
foundation drain because the technique was not widespread when they went up. Groundwater passes
through the wall and attacks the mortar rather than collapsing a pipe.

In Giffard, Vieux-Moulin and out toward Sainte-Thérèse-de-Lisieux, the stock changes completely:
bungalows on poured concrete, built between 1960 and 1985, all fitted with the standard clay tile
drain of the period. Those drains age together, and they fail together.

## What the geography adds

The borough slopes down toward baie de Beauport and follows the line of rivière Beauport. That
slope keeps the water table near the surface for a large part of the year. As long as the drain
works, nobody notices. The moment it stops carrying water, that water stays in contact with the
foundation longer than it would on drier ground, and the consequences show up sooner.

This is why the same symptom — a puddle at the base of a wall after snowmelt — does not lead to
the same diagnosis depending on whether you are up on boulevard Louis-XIV or lower down in the
borough, near boulevard Sainte-Anne.

## Before authorising any work

The useful reflex is documentary rather than technical: record the date, the location and the
weather conditions of each episode, photograph it, and insist on a diagnosis before an
excavation. The inspection report belongs to you, and it lets you compare two quotes against the
same observation rather than against two sales pitches.
