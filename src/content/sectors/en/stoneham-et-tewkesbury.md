Stoneham-et-Tewkesbury is not one foundation story but at least three, gathered under one
municipal name. Saint-Adolphe, Stoneham and Tewkesbury each have their own relationship to
their building stock and to water, and a diagnosis that ignores that split misses the point.

## A building boom compressed in time

The development of Vertmont-sur-le-lac in the mid-1970s pushed construction to roughly 85
units a year in the Stoneham sector. That pace produced a dense cohort of poured-concrete
foundations, laid over a corrugated PVC drain now nearing the end of its service life. That
wave, more than the village's overall age, explains how often shrinkage cracking turns up in
this sector.

## The old stone core, apart

In Saint-Adolphe, the pre-1945 stock remains a minority against that boom, but it follows a
different logic: those stone houses never had a perimeter drain, and what affects them is
water passing through mortar rather than a pipe collapsing. Treating a Saint-Adolphe house
like a Stoneham bungalow is chasing the wrong problem.

## A geography with three waterways

The territory slopes down toward rivière Jacques-Cartier, runs along lac Beaumont, and also
touches rivière Sainte-Anne and rivière des Hurons further north, toward parc national de la
Jacques-Cartier and réserve faunique des Laurentides. On lots where the grade concentrates
runoff toward one of these waters, a weakened drain lets water sit against the foundation
longer than elsewhere on the same territory.

## Before authorising any work

Given the mix of eras and foundation types across this territory, it is worth having an
inspection confirm which cohort a given property belongs to before accepting a quote. Document
the date of each episode, the exact location of the problem, and photograph it with a ruler
for scale: that report is what separates a Saint-Adolphe core house from a bungalow born of the
Vertmont-sur-le-lac boom.
