Saint-Sauveur does not share the unhurried building history of the borough's wealthier
neighbourhoods. It went up fast, between 1840 and 1900, to house workers from the shipyards
and the timber trade that once animated the nearby riverbanks — the reason municipal heritage
records still describe it as a historic working-class neighbourhood. That urgency marked the
foundation of every house still standing here.

## Built fast, built tight

The narrow lot is the neighbourhood's first constraint. Houses sit almost
touching, separated by what locals still call a rue étroite, a narrow street, rather than a
yard, and that closeness limits how much of a foundation a contractor can expose without
crossing onto the neighbour's property. This is not a question of building era, as in
Saint-Jean-Baptiste, but a question of space that never existed here.

## A wall that lets water through, not just a missing drain

Elsewhere in the borough, a missing drain is the central problem. In Saint-Sauveur, something
else has to be added: nineteenth-century construction with no fire-resistant standard, built
with less careful mortar, produced what local assessments call a permeable foundation — a
foundation wall permeable to water in its own right. Water does not just bypass a drain-less
foundation here; it passes through the stone in places, which changes how a damp patch in the
basement should be read. A diffuse stain that follows a mortar joint along its full length,
rather than a single point, is worth noting with the date and weather conditions before
calling in a professional.

## Rue Saint-Vallier Ouest, between houses and shops

Rue Saint-Vallier Ouest remains the neighbourhood's commercial spine, and the buildings
lining it share the same foundation as the houses on the side streets near église
Saint-Sauveur. An occupied storefront adds one more constraint to an inspection: access has
to work around a shop's hours, not only around the geometry of the lot.
