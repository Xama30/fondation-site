In 1934, surveyor Thomas Maher laid out the first lots on the southeastern shore of lac
Saint-Joseph. Twenty years later, 341 chalets already lined the shore; by 1960, the count reached
388. That resort subdivision, not an ordinary suburb, still defines half the housing stock of
Fossambault-sur-le-Lac today, dock and boathouse included.

## A foundation built for summer, lived in year-round

Chalets from that wave sit on stone or wood-pier footings, with no closed vide sanitaire and no
drain of any kind — the technique simply did not exist for this type of seasonal building.
As long as they served only in summer, the ground moisture had time to recede between stays.
Converted into year-round homes, heated and occupied through the winter, those same footings now
absorb a continuous moisture load they were never built to carry, and it is the mortar, not
poured concrete, that shows the effect.

## A water table the lake never lets drop

Lac Saint-Joseph is not a backdrop: it keeps the water table close to the surface all year along
the entire shoreline, from the first quai to the last. An undrained foundation, in that setting,
stays in contact with groundwater far longer than it would elsewhere. That factor, more than age
alone, explains why signs of wear tend to show up sooner here than in a comparable sector set
back from the lake.

## Two generations that do not compare

The handful of homes built since 2005 near route de Fossambault belong to an entirely different
generation: poured concrete, a drain meeting the BNQ 3661-500 standard, and a treed riverbank
often left intact well short of the foundation. The useful diagnosis for an old stone footing —
the state of the mortar, whether a pier has settled — has nothing to do with the one for a
ten-year-old foundation. Mixing the two up leads to a quote that is miscalibrated either way.
