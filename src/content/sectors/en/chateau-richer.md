Château-Richer does not have the mix of eras found in a neighbouring village. The municipality
stays farming and heritage-focused from one end to the other: a vineyard, orchards and cultivated
fields line avenue Royale, where the oldest stone and timber houses on the Côte-de-Beaupré sit
near Moulin du Petit-Pré, on rivière Sault-à-la-Puce.

## One cohort, one question

Where a more recent sector pits an old core against a 1970s subdivision, Château-Richer has
essentially one layer to read: stock predating 1945, some of it documented as far back as 1760,
built at a time when a foundation drain simply did not exist. The lime mortar between the
fieldstones, more forgiving than concrete, wears by crumbling rather than by cracking. The
question raised by a house on avenue Royale and the one raised by the barn next door are, from a
drainage standpoint, nearly the same question.

## What rivière Sault-à-la-Puce adds

Moulin du Petit-Pré was not built at random: it has drawn on the flow of rivière Sault-à-la-Puce
for generations, and that flow is still there today, a short walk from houses that predate the
mill's newest additions. For properties near this waterway, that same proximity keeps the water
table closer to the surface than elsewhere along avenue Royale, on ground that eventually drains
toward fleuve Saint-Laurent itself. Combined with an undrained foundation, water reaching the
mortar gets more chances to linger before it moves on, which speeds up joint wear on the homes
closest to the mill compared with those set farther back from the water.

## What sets an old wall apart from a modern foundation

The useful step starts with a simple observation: a joint that hollows out and crumbles between
fieldstones does not call for the same response as a vertical crack in a poured-concrete wall.
Noting the spot, the date, and photographing it with a ruler for scale documents the problem
before a professional determines whether the useful step is mortar repointing or something else.
