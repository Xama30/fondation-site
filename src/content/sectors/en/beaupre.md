Beaupré does not follow a single building cohort, which makes a standard diagnosis unreliable.
At the foot of Mont-Sainte-Anne, the ski resort that opened in 1968 brought condos and chalets in
successive waves, a pattern still continuing today with new residential projects. Farther down,
toward the mouth of rivière Sainte-Anne-du-Nord and along route 138, older permanent homes sit on
different ground, closer to fleuve Saint-Laurent.

## Two drainage logics, one municipality

A chalet built on the wooded slopes around Mont-Sainte-Anne sheds water down the natural grade of
the land: the slab and crawl space generally stay dry as long as nothing blocks the flow
downhill. A home built closer to the river or to rivière Sainte-Anne-du-Nord faces the opposite
problem: a water table the waterway keeps close to the surface for much of the year, especially
at snowmelt. These two logics coexist in the same town without either one dominating, which is
why no single cohort explains the whole building stock.

## From seasonal chalet to year-round home

Beaupré has a notable share of former chalets converted into year-round homes. A building
designed for occasional use was not always waterproofed to the standard of a house occupied all
year, and sustained use — constant heating, higher indoor humidity — eventually exposes
weaknesses in the footing that stayed hidden for decades. A woodpile stacked against an exterior
wall, common on this kind of property, can also trap moisture against the foundation without
anyone noticing.

## Observe before trusting a quote

Wherever the property sits, the first move stays the same: record the date and conditions of each
damp episode, photograph it with a ruler for scale, and insist on an inspection before
authorising work. That step separates a one-off problem from a failing drain, and it applies as
much to a condo near Mont-Sainte-Anne as to a house along fleuve Saint-Laurent.
