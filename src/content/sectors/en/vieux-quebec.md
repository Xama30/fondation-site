A stone house in Vieux-Québec never had a drain to lose: when its fieldstones went up, in the
17th and 18th centuries, the technique simply did not exist yet. That fact, true anywhere the
building stock reaches that far back, does not on its own explain what happens here: Vieux-Québec
adds a composite ground and a use that has become almost entirely touristic, two factors few
other areas combine.

## Footings set on two different supports

The walled quarter does not sit on uniform ground. Part of the stock, up on the cape, rests
directly on the bedrock near Citadelle de Québec; another part, lower toward the river, rests on
fill built up over centuries to gain habitable land. A fieldstone wall behaves differently
depending on which of those two supports carries it: bedrock does not hold moisture, old fill can
keep it against the stone far longer. Two neighbouring houses, a few metres apart, can therefore
age very differently without either one being poorly kept.

## What the tourist use changes

Few areas have seen their residential stock turn over this fast. Along rue du Petit-Champlain and
around Place Royale, basements that once housed a family now house an auberge touristique or a
location de courte durée unit. That conversion seals off cellars that used to breathe through
transoms now closed for soundproofing, changing how moisture escapes without a single pipe having
moved. Add the de-icing salt spread every winter on narrow, heavily walked streets like those
climbing toward terrasse Dufferin, and the lime mortar erodes faster at the surface.

## Before opening up a wall

The useful step stays documentary: date when a stain or a crumbling joint appeared, photograph it
with a ruler for scale, and get a written inspection report before authorising work on a wall
close to three centuries old. A fieldstone wall bound with lime mortar is not treated like a
poured-concrete foundation, and UNESCO World Heritage status often adds a municipal approval to
check before work can begin.
