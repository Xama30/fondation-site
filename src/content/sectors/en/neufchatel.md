Neufchâtel keeps its everyday name even though the municipal register renamed it Des Châtels in
2002. The sector forms a distinct L within the Les Rivières borough, bordered to the north by
avenue Chauveau and to the southeast by rivière Saint-Charles, and that shape explains why
pressure is not spread evenly across its territory.

## A name that changed, a sector that did not

West of boulevard de l'Ormière, the sector groups bungalows built between 1960 and 1980, the
dominant generation across the whole territory. The official name change did nothing to that
housing stock: it is still the same street pattern, including rue Durand and boulevard Bastien,
organising the neighbourhood around route 358.

## The southeast tip, against rivière Saint-Charles

It is there, where the sector touches rivière Saint-Charles directly, that the water table
pushes hardest against the original clay tile drains. Further north, along avenue Chauveau,
properties sit back from the water and do not see the same pressure on the drain. The
poured-concrete footing, though, cracks fairly evenly everywhere in the sector: uniform
shrinkage from the pour for this generation of construction, unrelated to how close a property
sits to the water. That is a useful distinction, because the same symptom — a puddle, a crack —
does not point to the same cause depending on where in the L you happen to be.

## Before authorising an excavation

The useful step stays documentary: note exactly where the water shows up, the date and whether
the episode follows heavy rain or snowmelt, photograph it with a ruler, and insist on a camera
inspection that tells a clogged tile apart from a normal shrinkage crack. That distinction
matters especially here, where the southeast tip and the north end of the sector do not share
the same pressure despite being the same age.
