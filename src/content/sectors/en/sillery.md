Sillery is not just its stately villa centenaire homes on chemin Saint-Louis, even though those
define the area's image. The territory stacks several land uses built in the same period, on the same type of
foundation, but exposed to very different pressures depending on exactly where you stand.

## A plateau that catches water from above

The homes built at the turn of the 20th century mostly sit on the plateau above the river cliff.
That elevated position does not remove the risk of water: it redirects it. Runoff coming from
higher ground uphill flows toward the rear wall of these houses rather than draining straight
toward the river, a path the local topography forces and that an undrained stone footing was
never built to intercept.

## What the boisé mature adds to the problem

Some of these properties still carry the boisé mature planted by the original estate-owning
families, often just a few metres from the foundation. Decades of growth have let roots reach the
lime mortar between the fieldstones, applying slow but constant pressure on joints that already
had no reinforcement. This factor sets Sillery apart from a denser area where street trees stay
well back from the walls.

## Institutional basements reopened

The former convents and presbyteries within the site patrimonial de Sillery, converted into
condominiums over recent decades, have had their basements reopened to create living units. Those
rooms had stayed closed, sometimes for a century: nobody was watching how the joints evolved.
Reopening them now exposes surfaces whose real condition was never tracked, which justifies a
closer inspection than on a house occupied continuously since it was built.

## Before authorising an excavation

The starting point stays documentary, whichever cause is at play: date when a sign appeared,
photograph it with a ruler for scale, and insist on a written report before any work begins. That
report also lets you check the contractor's RBQ licence against the official registry before
signing anything.
