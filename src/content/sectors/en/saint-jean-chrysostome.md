Unlike an area built all at once, Saint-Jean-Chrysostome was subdivided in waves spread across
nearly two centuries, which makes a generic diagnosis particularly risky here. Two neighbours
on the same street may well live in houses built fifty years apart, on foundations that share
nothing.

## Between two rivers, a patchwork of waves

The area is bounded on the west by rivière Chaudière and on the east by rivière Etchemin, and
construction proceeded through successive subdivisions since the parish was founded in 1828. No
single decade dominates enough to define one profile: streets such as avenue Taniata and rue
Chamonix sit next to older or newer stretches, without the kind of uniform cohort found in an
area built in a single wave. The parish itself took generations to become a town, a status it
only reached in 1965, long after the first farm lots were converted into residential streets.

## Farm drainage that no longer shows

Before residential subdivision, this land was farmed, and farmers had laid their own
field-drainage pipes to dry out cultivated soil. That abandoned agricultural network was not
always removed: it sometimes crosses the path of the newer french drain laid around
foundations on rue du Rucher, rue de la Sarriette or rue Fontaine, and a poorly documented
connection can cause a backup specific to this kind of converted farmland.

## What the lack of one cohort changes

Without a dominant construction wave, a diagnosis cannot rely on a permit's decade to guess a
drain's likely condition. Each case deserves its own check: note the date and exact location of
a backup, photograph it with a ruler for scale, and have a camera inspection confirm whether
the cause is a clogged modern drain or an old farm pipe never designed for the job it is now
being asked to do. Two neighbouring houses can get two different answers, precisely because
the area was never built to a single master plan.
