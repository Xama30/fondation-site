Montcalm shows rows of duplexes and triplexes today along Grande Allée and avenue Cartier, but
that face is almost always an add-on: the houses themselves date from the late 19th and early
20th century, built as grand single-family homes of British architectural influence. Nothing
about that original footing anticipated the subdivision that came later, which is why the
conversion itself, not the age of the stone alone, is where the useful questions start.

## Footings cut open to house more people

Converting a grand home into multiple units is never neutral for a stone footing. Serving a
basement unit requires access that didn't exist originally: a cage d'escalier commune, an
outside door cut straight through the fieldstones. Each opening made this way breaks the
continuous mortar joint the house had held for a century, creating a point of water entry the
rest of the wall, left intact, does not have.

## What the street trees add

The mature trees lining Grande Allée and avenue Cartier have been part of the landscape for
decades, planted close to a footing that never received a membrane because the technique did not
exist when it was built. Over time, their roots reach the same joints already weakened by the
conversion openings, layering two distinct pressures onto the same wall rather than just one.

## A commercial strip, not a diagnosis

The 34 abat-jour géants suspended over avenue Cartier mark the neighbourhood's commercial strip,
much like the nearby plaines d'Abraham place the sector on the map. Neither says anything about
the condition of a specific footing: it's the conversion history of the house itself, not its
commercial address, that determines where to look.

## Before authorising another opening

The useful reflex stays documentary: date any sign observed near a door or a stairwell added
after construction, photograph it with a ruler, and demand a written inspection report before
enlarging or altering an existing opening in a footing more than a century old.
