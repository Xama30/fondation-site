Charny owes part of its shape to two pieces of infrastructure, not just a cadastral survey: a
dam and a bridge. Understanding what each one did to the land helps place where a basement's
dampness comes from, well before thinking about a pipe.

## The dam that explains the water level

The core of Vieux-Charny formed along avenue des Églises, between the gare de Charny and the
hydroelectric plant (1901) on rivière Chaudière — older maps call it rivière Bruyante. The dam
feeding that plant holds the river's level just below the village, which keeps the water table
high year-round under stone foundations built before the french drain existed as a technique.
Groundwater passes through the wall and wears down the lime mortar, not a pipe that was never
there to lose. The riverside industrial buildings, mostly repurposed today, still mark the
footprint of this first working-class Charny, built around the rail line rather than the road.

## On the other side of pont Pierre-Laporte

The opening of pont Pierre-Laporte in 1970 shifted the centre of development toward a newer
Charny, built in poured concrete between 1975 and 1990, with a corrugated PVC drain laid around
the foundation. Nothing in common with the lime mortar of Vieux-Charny: this younger building
stock carries a structurally higher crack risk than a stone wall, even though its drain, when
it works, handles water the older area's high table never had to face in the same way.

## What an inspection needs to tell apart

A wet basement near avenue des Églises does not call for the same diagnosis as one near the
bridge: one comes from a drainless foundation under a dam-controlled table, the other from a
PVC drain that may simply have clogged with age. Record the date, the exact location and the
weather conditions of each episode, photograph it with a ruler for scale, and have an
inspection confirm which of the two mechanisms is at play before authorising anything.
