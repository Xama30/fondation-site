L'Ange-Gardien does not read as a single-era village. Facing fleuve Saint-Laurent and Île
d'Orléans, this farming municipality carries two building realities that grew up side by side
without either one taking over.

## A municipality with no dominant cohort

Along avenue Royale, barns and a handful of stone houses have stood since before 1945, built at a
time when a foundation drain did not exist. Closer to the river, newer homes sit on different
ground, with foundations designed differently from the start. Nowhere else on the Côte-de-Beaupré
is this lack of a majority cohort as clear: no single generation of building stock dominates the
territory, which changes how a diagnosis even gets framed.

## The farming past leaves marks on the housing stock

Part of L'Ange-Gardien's older stock was never designed as housing in the first place: a barn or
farm outbuilding converted into a residence often keeps the footing of its original use, with no
drain and no membrane, even after a full interior renovation. A neighbouring field or a
poorly placed hedge can also redirect runoff toward a single point on the lot instead of spreading
it out, a factor the newer stock closer to the river does not share in the same way.

## A case-by-case read

Since L'Ange-Gardien offers no single rule, the only reliable starting point is observing the
property itself: note where and when water appears, photograph it with a ruler for scale, and
have an inspection confirm whether the footing traces back to a farm building or a home before
authorising anything. Two neighbouring houses on the same stretch of avenue Royale can easily get
two different answers depending on the true age of their footing, which is why a generic quote
written without a visit is particularly unreliable in this specific municipality.
