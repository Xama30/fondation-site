In Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré, two neighbouring houses on avenue Royale do not raise the same drain
question, which is exactly why a generic quote is useless here. The old core, built before 1945
around the basilique Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré, sits on a stone foundation that never received a
drain. The belt of inns, motels and guesthouses added later along the same avenue often sits on
a younger foundation, with a real drain that can clog or crush like any other.

## Two ages of building, two different jobs

On a home in the old core, there is nothing to excavate to pull out an old pipe: the work means
installing a first perimeter drain, or treating mortar crumbling between the fieldstones. On a
newer inn or motel, the logic flips — a camera inspection has to confirm first whether the
existing drain is clogged or crushed before anyone proposes anything. A contractor who applies
the same diagnosis to both kinds of building has not really looked at the building.

## What running a business adds to the job

A motel or inn that stays open during the work faces constraints a private home never does:
guest access, parking, and noise near occupied rooms. A recurring damp basement in that setting
touches rented rooms, not just a storage space, which gives an operator extra reason to have a
clear work-phasing plan priced before signing.

## What to have in hand before signing

For an older home, confirmation that no drain already exists and the city's position on a
heritage permit. For an inn or motel, a camera inspection report and a written work-phasing plan
if the business stays open. The common thread in both cases stays the same: nothing gets
authorized before the diagnosis comes ahead of the excavation, and a quote silent on which of
the two jobs it is pricing has skipped the one question that actually decides the cost.
