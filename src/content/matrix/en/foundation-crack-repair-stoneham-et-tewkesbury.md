A foundation crack in Stoneham-et-Tewkesbury raises a question the region's urban sectors do not
ask in the same way: who is actually around to watch it? A good share of the housing stock, a
four-season chalet or a former cottage turned into a permanent home, is not occupied continuously,
and that directly changes how a crack gets documented before calling a contractor.

## Tracking depends on who lives in the house, not just the footing

A thin crack in a year-round home gets photographed easily at every change of season. In a chalet
visited every other weekend, that rhythm stretches out, and a crack that widens then closes again
with the frost can go unnoticed for months. The 1975-to-1990 poured-concrete cohort that dominates
the Stoneham sector is reaching the age where this kind of shrinkage crack becomes common;
documenting before every extended absence, not only in spring, keeps the record from losing its
thread.

## An open lot changes what a check costs in access

What the sector gives back is space. Most properties in Stoneham and Tewkesbury sit on large lots
with no shared wall and no neighbour pressed up against the house, which simplifies a targeted
excavation if the crack comes with a second sign and the drain needs checking. The old Saint-Adolphe
core is the exception: on its pre-1945 stone houses, what a contractor looks for is not a shrinkage
crack but crumbling mortar in a joint — a different read that has nothing to do with the
poured concrete found across the rest of the sector.

## What falls to the municipality, not Ville de Québec

Stoneham-et-Tewkesbury runs its own territory, its own zoning and its own permits through its own
311, separately from the Ville de Québec that surrounds it. Before authorising any excavation,
even a limited one, check with that office whether a permit applies — the answer depends on
proximity to the network, not on one rule for the whole Communauté métropolitaine.
