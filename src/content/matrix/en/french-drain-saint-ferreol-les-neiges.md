In Saint-Ferréol-les-Neiges, the french drain job that comes up most in a diagnosis is not
spread evenly across the municipality's two hamlets, nestled between mont Sainte-Anne and mont
Ferréol, near Canyon Sainte-Anne and Sept-Chutes. It concentrates around lac des Trois Castors,
in Saint-Nicolas, where a cohort of chalets built since this artificial lake was created in 1954
shares the same concrete-block foundation and the same clay-tile drain now at the end of its
life.

## A shoreline that sets its own rules

A property bordering lac des Trois Castors does not excavate the way a chalet set back in the
woods does. Proximity to the shore can bring a protective buffer into play, one only the
municipality's urban planning department can confirm, and that buffer sets how close excavation
is allowed to come to the water. A job planned without that confirmation risks having to be
redrawn partway through.

## From weekend chalet to a home heated all year

A meaningful share of the properties around lac des Trois Castors moved from seasonal use to
year-round occupancy without the foundation catching up to that shift. Constant heating in a
home lived in all year reveals weaknesses that stayed invisible through decades of chalet life,
and it is often at the point of that conversion that the original drain finally shows its
limits.

## What wooded access changes for the equipment

A chalet set back from the road, ringed by trees rather than served by a paved entrance, can
limit how wide the equipment reaching the foundation can be. That constraint does not apply the
same way in Saint-Léon, where the absence of a comparable artificial lake also removes the
constant pressure on the water table that Saint-Nicolas carries. A camera inspection report, a
written confirmation on the shoreline buffer where it applies, and a clear description of the
planned access: three items to demand before signing, whichever hamlet the property sits in.
