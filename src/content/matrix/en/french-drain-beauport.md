Replacing a french drain is the heaviest job on this list, and in Beauport it concerns an
unusually large share of the residential stock. The reason is documented in the area's own data:
the cohort built between 1960 and 1985 dominates the borough, and it received the standard clay
tile of the period.

## What changes from one street to the next

The diagnosis is often identical — a sixty-year-old drain whose sections have pulled apart — but
the job is not. A bungalow in Giffard or out toward Sainte-Thérèse-de-Lisieux can be excavated
around its full perimeter without particular difficulty. An older house on avenue Royale, on a
narrow lot inherited from the seigneurial survey, forces a different approach.

That detail of access is why two houses in the same borough, with exactly the same problem, get
quotes that look nothing alike. It is not arbitrary: it is the geometry of the lot.

## The lower borough works harder

Toward boulevard Sainte-Anne, rivière Beauport and baie de Beauport, the ground drops and the
water table stays near the surface for much of the year. A new drain there carries more water,
more often. That does not change the standard that applies, but it changes what an informed owner
asks for: a cleanout access designed in from the start, and a clear answer on the maintenance to
expect.

Higher up, toward boulevard Louis-XIV and autoroute Félix-Leclerc, the same bungalow cohort
reaches the same clay tile end-of-life without that additional hydraulic pressure.

## What to have in hand before signing

A camera inspection report, the standard and pipe type named, the city's position on the permit,
and an explicit line on restoring the property. Four verifiable items, none of which depends on
trust extended in advance.
