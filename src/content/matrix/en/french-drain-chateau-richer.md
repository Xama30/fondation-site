In Château-Richer, the question a foundation raises is almost never one of a drain reaching the
end of its run. The dominant stock in the municipality predates 1945, some of it dated to 1760,
built at a time when a perimeter drain simply was not a common technique. The job that comes up
here is, most often, a first installation against a stone wall that never had anything moving
water away from it besides the ground itself.

## A job that crosses cultivated land

Unlike an ordinary suburban lot, reaching a foundation along avenue Royale often means crossing
land still under cultivation. A vineyard, an orchard or a field borders much of the older stock,
and a neighbouring barn shares the same fate as the house: no original drain, the same gap to
fill. Excavation equipment has to work around planted rows, a narrower approach than a
subdivided lot allows, and sometimes a longer haul from the road to the wall.

## Heritage protection layers onto the usual permit

Part of the avenue Royale housing stock falls under heritage protection, which governs exterior
work beyond the standard building permit. Excavating around an old wall, even to fit a first
drain, can require an authorization separate from the usual one, and only the municipality's
urban planning department can confirm it. That step is not one a contractor can settle on the
owner's behalf.

## What to have in hand before signing

Written confirmation of the building's heritage status, if it applies, the municipality's
position on an excavation permit in an agricultural zone, and a clear description of how the
job reaches the site — from the road, across neighbouring farmland, or through an existing
right of way. Three items that set a Château-Richer foundation apart from most other areas this
service covers, and that a quote silent on all three has not actually priced the job in front of
it.
