On a pre-1940 stone foundation, the drain question in La Cité-Limoilou almost never comes down to
"is it clogged or crushed?" The cohort is so uniformly old, with no perimeter drain to begin with,
that the diagnosis nearly always points toward installing a first drain rather than replacing an
existing one. What actually varies from address to address is how many sides of the building a
contractor can reach at all.

## The party wall decides before the soil does

On a wall-to-wall triplex in Saint-Roch, Saint-Sauveur or Vieux-Limoilou, two of four faces
literally belong to the neighbouring building. A contractor cannot excavate them without crossing
the property line, which leaves the drain incomplete on those sides unless a written agreement
with the neighbour is in place — a negotiation that has nothing to do with the foundation itself.
A house that looks the same age with the same problem as one in Charlesbourg or Beauport gets a
different quote here for that geometry alone.

## The staircase and the yard, two more obstacles

The exterior staircase that defines the neighbourhood's look usually sits on the widest stretch of
facade, the one that would be simplest to excavate on a detached house. It sometimes needs
protecting, or a section removed for the duration of the job. Out back, the yard is often too
narrow to manoeuvre a standard-sized excavator, which pushes some contractors toward smaller —
and slower — equipment for that stretch.

## What that means for the quote

A quote that does not specify how many faces will be excavated, or what happens to the ones left
out, is pricing work it has not described. Ask for a simple perimeter sketch showing which faces
are covered, and whether tenants or a neighbour need notice before the work begins.
