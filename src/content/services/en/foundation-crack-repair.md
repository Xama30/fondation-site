Two cracks can be exactly the same width and have nothing else in common. One appeared the first
winter after the pour and has not moved since; the other has widened by a millimetre a year for
five years running and has finally thrown the door frame above it out of square. Filling both the
same way treats a symptom without knowing what causes it — and in half the cases, the crack
itself is not the problem, it is what the crack is announcing.

## What the shape of a crack tells you

A straight vertical crack in the middle third of a wall is most often tied to concrete curing
shrinkage: it shows up early, stays thin, and stops moving once the wall settles into itself. A
stair-step crack that follows the block joints, or one that widens toward the top or a corner,
tells a different story — a differential movement, one section of the wall dropping more than
another. A horizontal crack almost always signals lateral soil pressure against the wall, and of
the three, it is the least often harmless.

None of this reads reliably by eye without a reference point over time. That is why a contractor
who characterises a crack before touching it looks as much at how it has changed as at its shape
today.

## Why filling it alone settles nothing

A stable, non-structural crack is treated by injection from the inside — a relatively contained
job that does not require digging. But a crack that keeps moving has a cause that sits elsewhere,
often in a drain that no longer drains, or in soil that has lost bearing capacity under one
section of the footing. Injecting an active crack without addressing its cause closes the
symptom; the crack, or another one beside it, comes back.

That is why the step before any repair is not filling, it is reading: width, orientation,
movement documented over time, and whether or not a water trail is present. Two contractors who
propose the same fix without having asked the same questions are not actually proposing the same
thing.

## What separates a crack from a settlement problem

A single crack, even a wide one, is not automatically foundation settlement. The difference is in
scope: settlement usually shows up across several signs at once — a floor out of level, more than
one frame binding, more than one crack progressing in the same direction. A single crack, even an
active one, can stay a localized issue. It is the combination of signs, not any one of them alone,
that justifies bringing in an engineer before going further.
