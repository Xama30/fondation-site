A sump pump only enters the picture when an exterior drain, however well installed, cannot do the
job on its own: because water reaching the basement has nowhere to go by gravity, or because the
lot simply will not allow the perimeter to be excavated. It is a different system, with a
different weak point — and that weak point is neither the concrete nor the soil, it is
electricity.

## When gravity is not enough

A French drain works by gravity: water moves away from the foundation because the pipe slopes
toward a lower discharge point. That principle stops working once the basement sits below the
municipal line it would connect to, or once a shared party wall, an attached garage or a narrow
lot make excavating the perimeter unrealistic. In those cases, an interior drain runs along the
inside of the slab and channels water toward a pit, where a pump takes over the job gravity can
no longer do.

## Ongoing management, not something you close the book on

Once installed, this system is never really finished: unlike a replaced exterior drain, which can
be forgotten for decades, a sump system stays an active piece of equipment that depends on a
pump, a mechanical float switch and a working electrical connection at all times. Treating it
like a one-time repair leads to neglect — and neglect here is usually discovered on the day it
rains hardest.

## The weak point is electricity

The scenario that matters is not a pump wearing out after fifteen years of normal use — it is a
power outage during the storm that is filling the pit fastest. A contractor installing a sump
system should be able to say what happens in that exact scenario — a high-water alarm, an
independent second pump, a battery backup — before the question comes up on a night without power
rather than after. That is also what separates a system that was thought through from one that
merely functions: the second drains water as long as everything is working, while the first keeps
doing so, or at least warns you, on the day something fails.
