Foundation settlement stands apart from every other problem covered on this site in one specific
way: its cause is never in the concrete. A crack gets repaired, a drain gets replaced, a membrane
gets redone — those are all interventions on the foundation itself. Settlement, by contrast,
comes from the ground the foundation sits on, and it is that movement of the bearing soil that
decides everything that follows.

## What soil can do to a foundation

Soil loses bearing capacity in several ways: backfill that was never properly consolidated and
keeps settling for years after construction, clay that shrinks during a dry summer, a water table
whose level shifts and changes the pressure under the footing. The building does not respond the
same way everywhere — which is why the movement is almost always uneven from one section of wall
to another, producing the characteristic signs: a floor that goes out of level, a door frame
distorting upstairs, a crack that widens toward the top instead of staying uniform.

## Why the order of operations matters more here than elsewhere

On most foundation problems, the contractor makes the diagnosis and then acts on it. With
settlement, that step belongs to an engineer, not the contractor — and the order is not a
formality. Underpinning aimed at the wrong target, installed without knowing why the soil gave
way at that specific spot, can stabilize one section while the movement continues elsewhere under
the same foundation. The engineer's report is not an administrative step ahead of the work: it is
what determines where, and by which method, underpinning has to happen.

## What the contractor does once the cause is known

Once the mechanism is identified, the contractor chooses the underpinning method that matches
that specific mechanism, rather than a standard method applied by default. The job usually
involves both the soil and the structure, which is why it calls for a licence distinct from the
one covering excavation or wall waterproofing. Checking which one before signing avoids
discovering, once the work has started, that a subcontractor is missing from the site.

## What a homeowner can reasonably track in the meantime

None of this requires touching the building. A level held against a floor, a tape measure across
a door frame, and a dated photo of a crack are enough to build a timeline an engineer can use.
What matters is consistency: the same reference points, checked the same way, months apart. A
single observation says little about whether a foundation is still moving or has already settled
into its current position — a pattern over time says a great deal.
