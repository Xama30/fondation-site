Crushed-stone backfill placed under a concrete slab can contain pyrite — a mineral that, in the
presence of air and moisture, oxidizes and swells. That swelling pushes the slab upward and
cracks it from underneath. That mechanism is real and documented. What is far less established is
the idea that it should be suspected everywhere the moment a slab moves — and that is exactly
where most by-eye diagnoses go wrong.

## Why the region matters, not just the symptom

Pyritic backfill swelling is documented mainly for the greater Montreal area: that is where most
of the known cases and public records are concentrated. Nothing comparable, in a verifiable
public source, establishes the same thing for the Capitale-Nationale region.

Carrying that finding over here by geographic analogy — "it's a Quebec problem, so it must be a
risk at my address" — is not a cautious reading, it is a shortcut. That does not mean the risk is
zero here: it means the question — does the backfill under *this specific* slab contain it — has
to be answered case by case, in a lab, never by geography alone.

## What a crack pattern does not prove

A slab cracked in a star pattern, lifted at the centre, or partition walls binding against the
basement ceiling are real signs worth taking seriously. But they have several possible causes:
backfill that was never properly compacted and keeps settling, ordinary concrete shrinkage that
was unevenly distributed, water intrusion under the slab lifting the material locally. Pyrite is
one possibility among several, not the default first guess.

That is why the visual pattern is useful for asking the right question, not for answering it.
Answering it requires a sample of the backfill and a lab analysis under a recognized protocol —
the only way to measure swelling potential rather than guess at it.

## What follows a positive analysis

If the analysis confirms backfill at risk, the work consists of removing that backfill and the
slab covering it, then rebuilding on a stable material. It is work that touches both the ground
under the slab and the concrete structure itself, which is why the licence involved depends on
the exact scope of the job rather than being the same in every case.
