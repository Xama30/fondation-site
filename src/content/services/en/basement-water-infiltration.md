Water showing up at the base of a basement wall is not a diagnosis, it is a symptom. It tells you
a path exists between outside and inside; it does not tell you which one. Treating the spot where
water shows up rather than the path it travels is like sealing one outlet on a system that has
several — water blocked on one side finds the next. That is what sets infiltration apart from the
other subjects on this list: it is never itself the cause to treat, only the first observable fact
of a cause still to be established.

## One sign, several possible paths

A recurring puddle in the same spot can come from a drain that no longer drains, a crack in the
wall, a settled window well, or a garage door poorly sealed at the edge. These are four separate
problems, handled by different trades, at cost scales that have nothing in common with each
other. A contractor who quotes a price before establishing which of these paths is at fault has
not stated the problem yet — they have only offered an answer.

## Why treating one cause is not always enough

On a house where several components are nearing the end of their useful life at the same time, it
is not unusual for two causes to act together without one fully masking the other. A drain
losing capacity can let just enough water pressure build against the wall to start a crack
weeping that had been stable until then. Repairing the crack without looking at the drain treats
the most visible symptom and leaves the pressure climbing elsewhere, until the next sign appears.

## What the cause found changes

Once the path is identified, everything downstream changes: excavating and replacing a drain,
injecting a crack from the inside, correcting a window well, or installing an interior drain tied
to a sump pump when excavating the perimeter is not possible. These are different jobs, under
different licences, at budgets that are not remotely comparable — which is why the same
complaint, "water is coming in," can lead to very different quotes depending on what the
contractor actually observed.

While waiting for that visit, the useful step is the same no matter which path turns out to be
the cause: photograph where the water shows up, note the date and the conditions — sustained
rain, snowmelt, or both — and check whether the same spot repeats from one episode to the next.
That observation, made before the contractor arrives, becomes the first piece of the diagnosis
rather than a rough memory described on site.
