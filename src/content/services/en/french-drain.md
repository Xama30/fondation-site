  - q: "How long does a French drain last?"
    a: "It depends mostly on what it is made of. Houses built in this region between 1945 and 1975 generally received clay tile drains laid in short sections: those joints pull apart, fill with soil and eventually collapse. PVC drains laid after 1975 age better, but still clog wherever iron ochre is present."
  - q: "How do I know whether my drain is clogged or crushed?"
    a: "Not from outside, and that is exactly the point. A clogged drain can sometimes be cleaned; a crushed or disjointed one has to be replaced, which means excavating the perimeter. Only a camera inspection tells the two apart, and it costs a fraction of the excavation. Have it done before accepting a replacement quote."
  - q: "Should waterproofing be redone at the same time?"
    a: "This is the question that sets the real cost. Replacing a drain means excavating down to the footing, which exposes the buried wall. On a pre-1990 house, that wall is often protected by little more than tar, which no longer does much. Reopening the same trench two years later costs twice: ask for the two line items priced separately so you can decide with the numbers in front of you."
  - q: "My house is under five years old. Is this covered?"
    a: "Possibly, and do not have anything repaired before checking. The GCR warranty covers defects in design, construction or workmanship, and soil defects, for five years, and a failed foundation drain falls inside that window. Notice must be given within a reasonable time of discovery — case law treats that as six months. Repairing a covered defect yourself can compromise the claim."
  - q: "Which RBQ licence should I ask for?"
    a: "For excavation, subcategory 2.5. One detail almost nobody mentions: 2.5 sits under Schedule III, which the RBQ itself describes as not requiring an assessment of competence in carrying out construction work. Holding an excavation licence therefore attests to no assessed skill. Ask for the number, check it against the RBQ register, and ask who does the membrane — that is subcategory 7."

A French drain is not a basement accessory. It is the component that decides whether groundwater
leaves on its own or builds up against the foundation wall until it finds a way inside. While it
works, you never see it. When it stops working, you find out through a puddle at the base of a
wall — always the same spot — after sustained rain or during snowmelt.

## Why this is so common in the Quebec City region

The housing stock of the Communauté métropolitaine de Québec is old, and it is old in
concentrated waves. The big building periods — the suburban belts of 1960 to 1985, the
pre-1945 village cores along the river — were each built with the drainage technique of their
day. Mid-century houses got clay tile drains laid in short sections; pre-war houses often got no
drain at all, because the technique was not yet standard practice.

Those two cohorts arrive at the same place today by two different routes. That is why a problem
presented as exceptional is in fact the ordinary condition of a large share of the regional
building stock.

## Diagnosis comes before excavation, not after

This is where the difference between a few hundred dollars and several thousand is decided. A
drain that no longer drains may be clogged — by soil, by roots, by iron ochre — or physically
crushed, pulled apart, collapsed. The first can sometimes be dealt with without digging. The
second never can.

A camera inspection settles which it is, and its output — the recording and the report — belongs
to you. It also lets you compare two quotes against the same observation rather than against two
sales pitches. A replacement quote offered with no prior diagnosis is proposing a solution before
establishing the problem.

## What happens during the work

The contractor excavates the perimeter of the foundation down to the footing to expose the wall
and the old pipe. The new drain is laid on a stone bed, wrapped in its filter membrane, then
connected to its discharge point. Once the wall is uncovered, it is the natural moment to redo
the waterproofing — and the moment when that decision costs the least.

The job therefore involves at least two distinct trades, and often three. It is worth knowing
which of the people you are talking to holds which licence before you sign.
