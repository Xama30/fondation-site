A drain that clogs once was probably blocked by soil or roots, and a single cleaning settles the
matter for good. A drain that clogs a second time, then a third, only months after each cleaning,
points to something else: iron ochre, a deposit produced continuously by bacteria living in the
soil around the drain, not a one-time accident you close the book on.

## A continuous process, not a fixed deposit

Iron ochre is an orange, sometimes near-gelatinous gel that iron bacteria produce wherever iron
dissolved in groundwater meets oxygen — typically around a perforated drain, inside its joints
and perforations. Those bacteria do not die off just because their output has been removed: they
stay in the surrounding soil for as long as the conditions that support them remain in place,
which is to say almost always. That continuity is what sets ochre apart from a clog caused by
soil or roots, two problems that genuinely close after a single intervention. Orange-tinted water
at the drain outlet, or a rust-coloured deposit visible in the cleanout or the sump pit, are the
signs that let you tell the difference before the ground is even opened.

## Managed, not repaired

There is no intervention that removes iron ochre from the soil surrounding a drain: no treatment
permanently eliminates the bacteria responsible, and any claim otherwise is a promise that does
not hold up. What does exist is regular maintenance — preventive cleaning done before the drain
clogs completely, on an interval set by how severe the buildup has been. Treating ochre as a
problem you fix once almost always leads to the same disappointment: a drain that becomes
ineffective again just months after work sold as a permanent solution.

## What to require from a new installation

Part II of standard BNQ 3661-500 covers installation methods for a new drain, and this is where
confirmed ochre should change the quote you receive. An installation designed with maintenance in
mind builds in cleanout access at regular points along the run, rather than requiring a full
excavation every time cleaning is needed, and the pipe type chosen reflects that. A quote that
stays silent on the expected maintenance interval, or on how the drain will be accessed without
re-excavating everything, only solves half the problem — even when the drain itself is new and
compliant.
