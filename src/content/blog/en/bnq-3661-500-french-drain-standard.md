Iron ochre is the most poorly explained drainage problem in this region, and the line that turns
up most often in quotes — "BNQ-compliant drain" — is also the one that proves least. Not because
the standard is vague: because there are **two** of them, and the first comes in **two parts**
that do not cover the same thing.

## What the standard actually covers

**BNQ 3661-500** deals with *ochre deposits in building drainage systems*. It exists for a simple
reason that is rarely put this bluntly: those deposits can render a drainage system **completely
inoperative**. Not degraded, not slowed — out of service.

And it splits into two distinct parts:

- **Part I** covers **risk assessment** for ochre deposit formation in a new building, and
  **diagnosis** for an existing one;
- **Part II** sets out the **installation methods**, for new and existing buildings alike.

That split is not a standards-writer's nicety. If your house already exists, the part that
concerns you first is Part I — the diagnosis — and it is exactly the step a rushed quote skips on
its way to the excavator.

## The second standard, the one about the pipe

A **separate** standard, **BNQ 3624-130**, applies to the **perforated pipe** used for building
drainage, specifically to reduce the risk of ochre deposits forming.

This is where "BNQ-compliant" empties out. A contractor can install, using the methods in
3661-500 Part II, a pipe that does not meet 3624-130 — or the reverse. Both claims can be
separately true, and neither substitutes for the other.

## Why this matters especially here

Iron ochre is not an installation defect: it is a ground condition, tied to iron in the
groundwater and to what makes it precipitate on contact with the drain. One area can be exposed
to it and the next one not.

The direct and counter-intuitive consequence: **a new, correctly installed drain can clog within
a few years** if the risk was never assessed and the system was not designed to be maintained.
That is what Part I exists for. Replacing a drain without ever raising the ochre question means
accepting you might replace it twice.

## What you can demand, word for word

You need no technical knowledge to put these three requirements in writing in the quote.

1. **The standard number AND the part applied.** "BNQ 3661-500" on its own does not say whether
   the risk was assessed or whether this is purely about installation.
2. **The pipe type, and its conformity to BNQ 3624-130.** Named, not implied.
3. **Whether the ochre risk was assessed for your property**, and on what basis. If the answer is
   "we didn't look", that is a useful answer: it tells you the quote covers an installation, not a
   diagnosis.

A contractor who genuinely works to these standards answers effortlessly — they have them to
hand. A quote that stays on "compliant with BNQ standards" after those questions has told you
something too.

## The point to keep

Naming a standard is not proof; **the number, the part and the pipe type are**. It is verifiable,
it can be asked before signing, and it costs nothing. On a buried structure you will never see
again, it is very nearly the only check you have left.
