"Do I need a permit?" is the first question people ask about digging around a house, and it is
almost always the wrong one. In Quebec City the answer is usually **no** — and while everyone
watches the permit, the by-law that actually decides how good the work will be goes unread.

## What the planning by-law really requires

**R.V.Q. 102** lists the work that needs an authorization certificate. For excavation, article
**54, paragraph 9** covers cut, fill or excavation work carried out *on the bank of a watercourse
or a lake*. The trigger is not the excavation itself: **it is proximity to a shoreline.**

Article **57** then excludes excavation, cut and fill of a volume not exceeding **100 cubic
metres**.

A hundred cubic metres is a great deal of soil for a trench around a single-family house. Put
plainly: **an ordinary foundation excavation, away from water, does not usually trigger an
authorization certificate in Quebec City.**

Two qualifications that matter, and that nobody should improvise:

- **A lot is a specific case.** A riparian strip, a flood plain, a heritage sector or local zoning
  can change the answer. The planning department decides for a given address — not an article read
  from a distance, including this one.
- **This territory is not one city.** The Quebec City Metropolitan Community covers several
  municipalities, and Wendake comes under the Huron-Wendat Nation Council. The figures above are
  Quebec City's and do not transfer elsewhere.

## The by-law that actually bites: how the drain is connected

**R.V.Q. 2978**, on private connections and stormwater management, governs the work itself. It is
specific, and it is checkable in a quote.

**A foundation drain is never connected to the sanitary sewer.** Articles 33 and 34 permit only a
storm or combined system. Connecting to the sanitary line is not a matter of preference; it is a
compliance failure.

**In a new building, the connection is made indoors.** Article 36 requires the connection of the
foundation drain to the storm collector to be made inside the building by means of a deep
water-seal trap, fitted with a cleanout of at least **100 millimetres** in diameter. The same
article requires a check valve downstream on the storm collector, to stop stormwater backing up
into the foundation drain.

**In an existing building already connected outdoors**, the drain may stay outdoors when it is
replaced. It then connects directly to the storm branch, without a trap — but it requires **an
access chimney 100 millimetres in diameter located near the foundation**.

**Where gravity is not enough**, article 37 requires an indoor system with a holding basin and
pumping. And where there is no storm collector at all, article 38 sends the discharge to a
stormwater management structure or a ditch — never to the sanitary sewer.

## Why those two figures are worth more than a permit

The cleanout and the access chimney look like plumbing trivia. They actually decide what will be
possible ten years from now.

A buried drain can only be re-inspected through an access point. **Without a chimney, a drain that
silts up can be neither camera-inspected nor flushed** — the only remaining option is to dig it up
again. This is exactly where the by-law meets the iron ochre question, whose standard likewise
calls for maintenance access. A compliant drain is a drain that can be maintained.

## What to ask for in writing, before signing

None of this needs technical knowledge, and a contractor who works in the region answers without
effort.

1. **Which system the drain will discharge to** — storm, combined, or pumped. If the answer is
   "sanitary", the conversation ends there.
2. **Where the connection is made**, indoors or outdoors, and why in your case.
3. **Whether a 100 mm access chimney is planned near the foundation**, and whether it is in the
   price or extra.
4. **Whether a check valve is included**, and what it is fitted to.

Ask as well that the quote **name the municipal by-law it works to**. A company that works in
Quebec City knows it; a company arriving from elsewhere may discover the indoor-connection rule at
the same time you do.

## The thing to take away

The permit is the wrong worry. What decides how long the work lasts sits in a connection by-law
almost nobody reads: **where the water goes, how you reach it, and what stops it coming back**.
All three are public, they are asked before signing, and checking them costs nothing.
