A basement taking on water makes you want to act fast. On a recent house, that is precisely the
instinct that can cost you the most — not because of the work itself, but because **having a
potentially covered defect repaired yourself can compromise the claim**.

## Three windows, and only one that reaches a foundation

Quebec's new residential building warranty plan does not cover everything for the same length of
time. It reads in three blocks:

- **1 year** — existing, non-apparent poor workmanship, from acceptance of the building.
- **3 years** — latent defects, from acceptance of the building.
- **5 years** — **design, construction or workmanship defects, and soil defects**, from the end
  of the work.

The third one is what concerns you here. A badly installed drain, a wall that is moving, ground
whose behaviour was never accounted for: those are design, construction or soil defects, and the
window is **five years**, not one.

Plenty of owners give up because they have "the one-year warranty" in mind. The one-year warranty
is real. It simply is not about this.

## Why repairing first can close the door

A claim rests on the defect being **observable**. Once the wall has been excavated, the drain
replaced and the ground reinstated, what there was to see is gone, and you are asking a third
party to accept your word and your invoices as evidence.

Hence the order that protects your rights, which is the opposite of instinct:

1. **Report it first.** The step is administrative; it demands no technical diagnosis from you.
2. **Have it documented.**
3. **Only then decide** what work you have carried out.

None of this stops you acting in a genuine emergency — but it changes the order you do it in, and
it costs only a few days.

## The deadline: "reasonable", and what that means

The problem must be reported within a **"reasonable time"**. The text sets no number of days; it
is **case law that has established that period as six months from discovery**.

The distinction matters, and it cuts both ways. This is not a regulatory deadline that can be
held against you mechanically — but neither is it an elastic one. Six months after noticing, the
position becomes hard to defend.

So note **the date of discovery**, not just the date of the work. That is what starts the clock,
and a timestamped photograph beats a recollection.

## It follows the house, not the buyer

The warranty is **transferable**. If you bought a three-year-old house, you are not out of the
running: coverage runs from the end of the original work, not from your purchase.

It is a check that takes minutes and that almost nobody makes before calling a contractor. It
does assume that **the original contractor was accredited** with the plan — that is the first
thing to confirm.

## What to actually do

Before signing anything on a house under five years old:

- **establish the date the original work ended**, not the date you bought;
- **check whether the building is covered** by the warranty plan;
- **report it in writing** if you believe it is, before authorising any excavation;
- **date and photograph** what you are seeing, with a ruler if it is a crack.

Getting a foundation quote is still a good idea — but after those four steps, not before. If
coverage exists, you may just have saved the cost of the work. If it does not, you have lost a
few days and gained a documented file, which you will need anyway to compare two quotes against
the same observation.
