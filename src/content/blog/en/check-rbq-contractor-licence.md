"We're RBQ licensed." It appears in almost every foundation quote, and it is almost always true.
That is not the problem. The problem is that an RBQ licence is not a single object: it is a set
of **subcategories**, and a contractor holds some of them and not others. Two minutes of checking
tells you which.

## A foundation job often touches three subcategories

Replacing a french drain around a house is not one trade. It is three, following each other along
the same trench.

- **2.5 — Excavation and earthwork.** The official text covers "digging, moving, compacting,
  levelling earth or granular materials". This is what allows someone to open the ground against
  your wall.
- **3.2 — Small concrete works.** It explicitly names "concrete formwork for the footings and
  foundation walls of buildings covered by Part 9 of the National Building Code". Part 9 is the
  single-family house. So **3.2 is the subcategory that covers your foundation wall, not 3.1**,
  which covers structural concrete.
- **7 — Insulation, waterproofing, roofing and exterior cladding.** This is the one that covers
  waterproofing, and therefore the membrane applied to the wall once it has been exposed.

A contractor can perfectly well hold 2.5 and not 7. They would be entitled to dig down to your
footing and not entitled to waterproof the wall they have just uncovered. Nothing in the sentence
"we're RBQ licensed" tells you that.

## The fact nobody writes down: digging requires no assessed competence

Subcategories are sorted into schedules, and the schedule changes everything. Of the ones in
**Schedule III**, the Régie writes:

> These subcategories do not require an assessment of competency in carrying out construction
> work.

**2.5 — excavation — is in Schedule III.** So are 3.2 and 7. Holding those licences shows that
the company has met administrative and solvency requirements, not that an examination has
verified its competence to carry out the work.

By contrast, **2.6 — Piles and special foundations** falls under **Schedule II**, which does
require that assessment. Its official text names "soil mechanics, such as piles and caissons,
excavation shoring, anchor rods, **underpinning**, or grouting in soil and rock".

That is the most useful distinction in this whole article. If your problem is **settlement** — a
house that is moving, not a wall that is seeping — the relevant subcategory is 2.6, and it is the
only one of the four whose competence in execution has actually been assessed. A contractor
offering to underpin a foundation on a 2.5 is working outside their scope.

## How to check, in practice

The Régie keeps a public, free register of licence holders. The process takes four steps, none of
which requires any technical knowledge.

1. **Ask for the licence number in writing**, in the quote. A refusal, or an "I'll send it over"
   that never arrives, is already an answer.
2. **Look the number up in the register.** Confirm the licence is **in force** — not suspended,
   not expired.
3. **Read the subcategories listed** and compare them against the work being proposed. This is
   the step almost nobody takes, and the only one that teaches you anything.
4. **Check that the name on the register is the one signing the quote.** A licence belongs to a
   company; a number borrowed from a related company does not cover you.

## What this changes about the conversation

You do not need to argue technique to use any of this. One question is enough, and it is
verifiable: *which subcategories cover the work you are proposing, and who carries out the part
you do not cover?*

A serious company answers without hesitating, because the answer is public anyway. A company that
subcontracts the waterproofing will tell you so, and that is entirely normal — what is not normal
is finding out after the work is done.

This check costs nothing, happens before you sign, and depends on no technical opinion. It is the
only action this page asks of you, and it is the one that pays best.
