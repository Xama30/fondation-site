The question comes in two forms. Someone building a house wants to know whether they can skip it.
Someone buying a 1950s house with no drain wants to know whether they are breaking a rule. **The
two answers are not the same**, because the rule targets what gets built, not what already
stands.

## For new construction: yes, unless shown otherwise

The Quebec Construction Code, Chapter I – Building, adopts the 2015 National Building Code with
Quebec amendments. For small buildings, its article **9.14.2.1** states, in substance, that unless
it can be shown to be unnecessary, the bottom of exterior foundation walls must be drained by
pipes or drains laid outside the foundation, or by a layer of gravel or crushed stone.

So the drain is the rule and its absence the exception. The technical sheet published by GCR, the
body that administers the new-home warranty plan, spells out what "shown" means: **a written
opinion from a professional**, in a sealed and signed geotechnical report, after checking the site
conditions. The contractor's opinion is not enough.

The same sheet points out a consequence people forget: the Code's ready-made solutions for footings
and foundation walls assume **drained soil**. Without drainage, the foundation has to be designed
by an engineer, with sealed plans. Skipping the drain does not simplify the job; it moves it into
engineering.

Drainage is required all around the foundation, whether there is a finished basement, a crawl
space or nothing at all behind the wall.

## Who enforces the rule on a house

For a single-family house, the Régie du bâtiment does not enforce the Code itself: small
residential buildings are exempt from its enforcement, and the municipality's construction by-law
applies. For a new house covered by the warranty plan, GCR relies on this same article, as its
technical sheet shows.

## For an existing house: the rule applies to the work

None of the texts consulted here requires the owner of an existing house without a drain to
install one. What changes things is the moment work is done.

In Quebec City, **R.V.Q. 2978** applies as soon as the drain is touched:

- **a permit is required to install a foundation drain** (article 61), covered in
  [the piece on permits](/en/blog/foundation-drain-connection-rules-quebec-city/);
- on a new building, the drain is **connected inside**, through a trap with a cleanout of at least
  100 mm (article 36);
- a drain that cannot flow by gravity goes to a **sump with a pump** (article 37);
- a **backwater valve** must protect the drain against backflow (article 48).

In other words: nothing forces you to dig, but if you dig, the drain you lay has to meet these
rules, and the quote should name them.

## When to add a drain to a house that has none

Having no drain is not a defect in itself: many older houses in the region have had a dry basement
for decades. The signs decide, not the age:

- water at the foot of basement walls after a thaw or a heavy rain;
- white deposits (efflorescence) or damp patches rising on the concrete;
- cracks that let water in;
- grading that sends water back toward the house.

Before paying for an excavation, have someone establish where the water comes from. A downspout
emptying at the foot of the wall or a lot sloping toward the house can be fixed without a drain.
